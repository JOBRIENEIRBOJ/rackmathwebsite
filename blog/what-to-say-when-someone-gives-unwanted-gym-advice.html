What to Say When Someone Gives Unwanted Gym Advice

Learn what to say when someone gives unwanted gym advice, with simple scripts to stay calm, protect your workout, and keep building beginner gym confidence.

You are in the middle of a set, trying to remember your form and count your reps.

Someone walks up and starts telling you what you are doing “wrong.”

Now you are not thinking about the weight. You are thinking: *What do I even say right now?*

The simple truth

Unwanted gym advice happens to almost everyone.

You do not have to argue. You do not have to follow their tips. You do not have to let it wreck your workout.

You just need a simple, polite script and a way to get back to what you were doing.

Why this matters

Feeling watched or judged is one of the big reasons people get gym anxiety and avoid the weight room.

That is a problem, because strength training is part of the kind of physical activity major health organizations recommend for adults at least two days per week to support overall health and function.[^1][^2]

If weird social moments keep pushing you out of the gym, it gets much harder to build that habit.

So it is worth having a plan for the awkward human stuff, not just the sets and reps.

What beginners usually get wrong

Most beginners do one of three things:

1. Freeze. They nod, say nothing, and then their brain replays the moment all day.

2. Do whatever the person said, even when it feels wrong. Now they are lifting in a way they do not understand, which can actually make training harder to manage.

3. Avoid that area of the gym next time. Or avoid the gym completely.

None of this means you are weak or “too sensitive.” It just means you were surprised and did not have a script ready.

Let’s fix that.

What to do instead

Here is a simple plan you can run every time:

### Step 1: Decide in advance what you want

Before you walk in, pick one of these goals for the day:

  • “I’m here to practice my plan, not change it.”
  • “I’m here to stay calm and finish my workout.”

When you know your goal, it is easier to see random advice as background noise, not a command.

### Step 2: Use a short, polite script

You do not owe strangers your training details or your medical history. You just need a calm, boring sentence.

Pick one of these and practice saying it in your head:

If you want to shut it down fast:

  • “Thanks, I’m good for today.”
  • “Appreciate it, I’m following a plan right now.”

If you want to be extra neutral:

  • “Thanks for looking out. I’m working on this with a coach / program.”
  • “Got it, I’m going to finish this set as planned.”

If they keep talking:

  • “I really need to focus on this set. Have a good workout.”
  • “I’m going to get back to my plan now, thanks though.”

Say it once. Then look back at your bar, your dumbbells, or your phone timer—not at them.

Your body language can quietly say: “Conversation is over.”

### Step 3: Know when to ask for real help

Not all advice is bad.

Sometimes staff or trainers are trying to keep you safe, especially if something looks risky.

If a staff member says something like, “Hey, can I show you a safer way to rack that bar?” it can be worth listening.

When safety is involved, a simple response is:

  • “Sure, I’m still learning. What should I change?”

Then you can decide later if you want to keep that change or get a second opinion from a trusted coach, physical therapist, or experienced friend.

### Step 4: Protect your form and your body

Random advice can push you into using weights or movements you are not ready for.

For beginners, it is usually better to:

  • Use a weight you can control with good form.
  • Move through the exercise in a controlled way.
  • Stop the set if something feels sharp, pinchy, or just wrong for your joints.

Major groups like the American College of Sports Medicine recommend strength work that is appropriate for your level and progressed over time, not all at once.[^2]

So if someone is telling you to “go heavier” and your form is already shaky, it is okay to ignore that.

You are the one holding the weight.

### Step 5: Have a mental “reset” after the interaction

Unwanted advice can throw off your focus.

After the person walks away:

1. Take a slow breath in through your nose and out through your mouth. 2. Glance at your plan or notes: *What set and rep was I on? What lift is next?* 3. Do a lighter “reset” set if you need to get your head back in the movement.

Then move on. Do not let one awkward moment steal the rest of your workout.

Simple scripts you can steal

You can copy these straight into your phone notes:

  • “Thanks, I’m good for today.”
  • “Appreciate it, I’m following a plan right now.”
  • “Thanks for looking out. I’m working on this with a coach / program.”
  • “I really need to focus on this set. Have a good workout.”
  • “I’m going to get back to my plan now, thanks though.”

If you are nervous, literally read from your notes the first time. People talk to their phones at the gym all the time. No one will care.

How RackMath helps

One sneaky reason unwanted advice feels so stressful: you are already juggling a lot in your head.

  • What weight should I use?
  • How many sets are left?
  • Did I already do this side?
  • Is my form okay?

When you are new, even plate math is another thing pulling your attention away from staying calm and confident.

RackMath helps by:

  • Telling you exactly what plates to put on the bar, so you are not standing there doing math while someone watches.
  • Tracking your sets and weights, so when someone says “you should go heavier,” you can quietly think, *Actually, I’m progressing fine. Last week I did 65, today I’m at 70.*

It does not fix rude people. But it does remove one layer of stress, so it is easier to stick to your plan and ignore random advice.

Final thought

Awkward moments in the gym do not mean you do not belong there.

They mean you are a person, around other people, in a place with a lot of opinions.

Have a plan. Have a sentence ready. Then get back to your bar and do the work you came to do.

You are allowed to take up space in the weight room, even while you are still learning.
