When Should You Increase the Weight?

Know when to increase lifting weight with a simple rep-range example, sensible load changes, and a workout log that makes your next step easier to choose.

Build your reps, add a little weight, then build your reps again.

If your workout gives you a rep range, that is a simple way to decide when to move up. Increase when you can reach the top of the range on every working set, with good control and another good rep or two left.

If your coach or program already gives you a progression rule, follow it. The approach below is one practical option for a general lifting routine.

Start with the rep range you already have

Suppose your bench press calls for three sets of 8 to 12 reps. These are your working sets; warmup sets do not count toward the three.

You use 95 lb and complete 10 reps, then 9, then 8. All three sets are within the range. Keep the same weight next time and see whether you can add a controlled rep somewhere.

Eventually, you complete 12, 12, and 12. Your range of motion stays consistent, and you finish each set feeling that you could probably manage another one or two good reps. That is a reasonable point to consider a small increase.

You do not need to attempt those extra reps to prove they are available. It is a rough check on effort. You can make progress without pushing every set until you cannot finish another rep.1

If you are unsure whether you could repeat it, use the same weight once more before moving up.

Here is what that can look like in a log

Here is an example, not a schedule you need to match. You might repeat any row for several workouts. Assume the exercise, technique, rest periods, and equipment stay comparable.

Example sessionTotal barbell weightReps across three setsNext step
195 lb10, 9, 8Keep 95 lb and build reps
295 lb11, 10, 9Keep building reps
395 lb12, 12, 12Consider a small increase, or repeat to confirm
495 lb12, 12, 12Try a manageable increase
5100 lb9, 8, 8Keep 100 lb and build reps again

The lower rep count after adding weight is expected in this example. You have made the exercise harder while staying inside the planned range.

If the new weight produces 8, 6, and 5, it is too large a jump for this particular plan today. Return to the previous load or use a smaller increase if the equipment allows it.

Make the jump small enough to use

For a barbell, a 5 lb increase in total weight means adding 2.5 lb to each side. With kilogram plates, adding 1.25 kg per side increases the total by 2.5 kg.

Check the total with the barbell calculator, or use the examples in how to calculate plates on a barbell.

The size of a jump matters relative to what you are lifting. Moving from a 20 lb dumbbell to a 25 lb dumbbell is a 25% increase per hand. Moving a 100 lb barbell to 105 lb is 5%. The same five-pound label can represent a very different challenge.

If your gym's next dumbbell is a large jump, use an intermediate size if available. Otherwise, continue improving within your current rep range and discuss a suitable adjustment with your coach if you keep getting stuck. Repeating the current weight is reasonable while you work out a manageable next step.

Keep the reps comparable

Use the same range of motion, similar control, and roughly the same rest between sets when deciding whether to increase. Twelve shortened reps tell you something different from twelve reps through your usual range.

Check all the working sets. Reaching 12 on the first set while the others remain at 9 and 8 gives you room to keep improving at the current weight.

Extra reps are real progress. Research supports increasing repetitions as another way to build strength and muscle, so you can improve before the weight changes.2

If a heavier weight causes new or sharp pain, stop that set and get appropriate guidance before trying it again.

Use the next workout to learn more

One disappointing session does not erase your previous work. Log the result, note anything different, and compare another similar session before deciding that your progression has stalled.

Keep a record of weight, reps for each set, and a short note about effort or technique. A change from 10, 9, 8 to 11, 10, 9 becomes easy to see, even while the weight stays the same. Our guide to how personal records work explains how to recognize those smaller improvements too.
