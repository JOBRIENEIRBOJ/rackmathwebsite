How PRs Work: What Counts as Progress?

Learn what a lifting PR means, how rep and weight records differ, and how to judge progress without expecting a new personal best every week.

A PR is a personal record: your best recorded result at a particular task. PB, or personal best, means the same thing. In lifting, the useful question is: your best result at what, exactly?

Doing more repetitions with the same weight can be a PR. Lifting more weight for the same number of repetitions can also be a PR. You can recognize either achievement without attempting the heaviest single lift you can manage.

Decide what you are comparing

Suppose your best bench press set at 135 lb is eight repetitions. You later complete nine, using the same technique and moving the bar through the same distance. That is a rep PR at 135 lb.

Now suppose 135 lb was the most you had bench pressed for five repetitions. Completing five at 140 lb is a five-rep weight PR. The number of reps stays fixed; the weight increases.

Keep the exercise, equipment, technique, and range of motion comparable. Range of motion simply means how far you move through the exercise. A shallower squat or a bench press helped by a spotter changes the task. Record what happened rather than treating it as an identical comparison.

For these examples, assume the earlier result was your previous best for that specific target:

Earlier resultNew resultWhat it tells you
135 lb for 8 reps135 lb for 9 repsA rep PR at 135 lb, with comparable technique.
135 lb for 5 reps140 lb for 5 repsA new best weight for five reps.
135 lb for 8 reps140 lb for 5 repsMore weight, fewer reps. Check your five-rep and heaviest-weight records separately.
135 lb for 8 reps135 lb for 8 reps with better controlUseful technique progress; the numerical record stays the same.

The third row is especially easy to misread. If 140 lb is the heaviest you have ever lifted, it can be a heaviest-weight PR. But it does not automatically beat your eight-rep performance or prove your one-rep maximum increased. Name the record so you know what improved.

A PR does not have to be a one-rep max

Your one-rep max, usually written 1RM, is the heaviest weight you can lift once using the chosen exercise technique. A tested one-rep record and an estimated one-rep max are different entries in a training log.

An estimated one-rep max calculator uses a set's weight and repetitions to calculate a possible maximum. It cannot establish that you actually lifted that weight. Estimates depend on how well a formula fits your performance; people differ in the relationship between weight and repetitions.1

Label the number "estimated" and keep the original set beside it. For everyday progress tracking, "135 lb for nine controlled reps" is already a clear, useful result.

How often should you set a PR?

You do not need to produce a new record every week. When you are learning an exercise, improvements may come frequently. With more training experience, new bests can become smaller and less regular. Your training plan and recovery also matter.

Treat your log as a sequence of workouts. For example, eight, eight, seven, then nine reps at the same weight includes a new best even though the path was uneven. Those numbers are an illustration, not a schedule to follow.

Regular training can move you forward without a record at every session. ACSM's guidance supports progression tailored to the person, and you do not have to push every set until another repetition is impossible.2

What is a healthy increase?

There is no single healthy percentage increase per month that applies to every lift and person. A useful next step should fit the repetitions you intend to perform and the technique you can maintain.

That step might be one additional controlled rep. For a barbell exercise, it might be 2.5–5 lb added to the total bar weight, if that suits the lift and your current performance. With kilogram equipment, a 2.5 kg total increase would mean adding 1.25 kg per side. Smaller steps can be useful when equipment allows them.

For example, adding a 2.5 lb plate to each side takes a 135 lb barbell to 140 lb. Whether you are ready for that jump depends on the exercise and how the current sets are going. Keeping the weight unchanged remains an option.

For a practical decision process, read when to increase the weight you lift.

Keep enough detail to recognize progress

Record the exercise, equipment, weight, reps in each set, and approximate rest between sets. Add a short note when something changes: "paused reps," "different machine," or "last set after a longer rest."

Look back over several comparable workouts before deciding you are stuck. Repeat testing can vary even under controlled conditions.1 A single disappointing set gives you limited information.

If progress stays flat, review your program and recovery, or ask a qualified coach to help identify an appropriate adjustment. A calendar deadline is a poor reason to force a heavier attempt. Give yourself a clearly defined next step and record it honestly when it happens.

Reading your records in RackMath

Use History to look back at your logged weights, reps, and dates. For eligible main lifts, Insights → Personal Records shows Max Weight and Est. 1RM. The estimated max is calculated from logged sets; it is not proof that you have lifted that number for one rep.
